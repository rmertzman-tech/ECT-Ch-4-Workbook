1 · Arrive

Start with dignity, not usefulness

In Chapter 4, you learned that people are not merely instruments for someone else’s goal. We rely on one another all the time, but ethical use must preserve agency, consent, privacy, and dignity.

Privacy reminder: You may use the provided case, a fictional example, a public example, a historical example, a composite example, or a low-stakes personal example. Do not disclose private, identifying, or sensitive information.
2 · Read the Case

The Powerful Story

Case: A student is writing a persuasive essay about housing insecurity. They know a classmate who once described a frightening experience with eviction, family stress, and temporary homelessness. The story would make the essay much stronger. The classmate once said, “You can talk about it if it helps people understand,” but that conversation was informal and private. The student now wants to use vivid details in a class presentation and maybe post the essay online later.

The student thinks the cause is important. They also realize the story is not only “evidence.” It belongs to a person who may have boundaries, risks, and a standpoint of their own.

3 · Person-as-Agent

Who is the person, not just the tool?

Person-as-agent: a person with a standpoint, choices, vulnerability, boundaries, and some capacity to participate in what happens to them.
4 · Use vs Mere Use

Apply the Use vs Mere Use Test

Use vs mere use: We may use one another’s help, work, stories, or roles respectfully. Mere use happens when someone’s agency is bypassed and the person is treated only as material for another goal.
5 · Consent, Pressure, Privacy

Check the agency conditions

Consent: meaningful agreement based on understanding, real choice, and a genuine option to refuse.
Privacy: a dignity-protecting boundary around information, images, stories, identity, and exposure.
6 · Dignity and Limits

Do not overclaim

Chapter 4 requires respect and humility. A person’s story may be powerful, but the power of the story does not prove permission, safety, or dignity.

7 · Repair

Choose a dignity-preserving repair

Repair should restore or protect agency. It should make refusal possible, reduce exposure, and avoid turning apology into an afterthought.
