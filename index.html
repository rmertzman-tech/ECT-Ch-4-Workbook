Feeling certain is not the same as being right

2 · Bishop Saad Sirop Hanna

Twenty-eight days

Saad Sirop Hanna was a Chaldean Catholic priest in Baghdad. On August 15, 2006, after celebrating Mass, he was kidnapped by militants. He was held for twenty-eight days — beaten, held blindfolded in solitary confinement, subjected to a mock execution at a riverbank. He attempted escape by swimming the Tigris River, was recaptured, and was severely punished for it.

Throughout, his answer to his captors' central demand did not change:

"I will not become a Muslim, and I fully know that you are going to kill me for it."

He was released on September 11, 2006, after a ransom effort mobilized by Catholic and Chaldean Church leadership across multiple countries — significant enough that Pope Benedict XVI publicly prayed for his release. In 2009, despite the danger, Hanna returned to Iraq to continue serving Iraqi Christians. He was consecrated bishop in 2014.

This case is not offered as proof that individual conviction alone is enough to survive anything. Hanna's survival depended on family standing, Church institutional resources, and an international mobilization most people do not have access to.

What does this case ask you not to conclude from it — and why does that caution matter?

3 · The Suppression Gap, at Its Most Extreme

Raw capacity vs. permitted expression

Hanna's raw capacity — his convictions, his composure, his sense of who he was — did not waver once across twenty-eight days. What collapsed was not his capacity. It was his permitted expression: his ability to act on that capacity, to reach anyone outside the room he was held in.

Why are "what a person could do" and "what a person is currently permitted to do" not the same measurement?

4 · BioBond, Tested

Chapter 1's definition, at its hardest test

BioBond: a relationship, community, or source of support that changes what a person's capacity can reach in the world, without changing the capacity itself. Church mobilization for Hanna's ransom did not make him braver — it changed what his composure could actually accomplish.

Hanna needed both his own composure and the Church's mobilization. Why couldn't either one have substituted for the other?

5 · Kant's Humanity Formula

Tested against its hardest case

The demand made of Hanna was direct: convert, or be killed. In Kant's terms, this was a demand to treat his own conscience — his capacity to hold a conviction that was genuinely his — as a means to someone else's end, with his life used as the leverage to force the exchange.

Why does Kant's rule say a person's rational agency may never be used this way, regardless of what the person demanding it believes they will gain?

7 · Coherence Is Not the Same Thing as Truth

What total certainty actually proves

A belief system that is completely internally coherent will always feel, from the inside, like it simply describes reality. This feeling is not, by itself, proof that it matches the world outside it.

What would it actually take for you to tell the difference between your conviction feeling coherent to you, and your conviction actually matching the world outside you?

8 · Assembly History

Why someone else's certainty looks unreasonable to you

Every person's Assembly History — the specific sequence of experiences that built their PRF — is different, which is why equally coherent people can hold genuinely different convictions with equal certainty.

Think of someone whose deepest convictions differ sharply from yours. What might their Assembly History have been, that made their view feel as obviously coherent to them as yours feels to you?
